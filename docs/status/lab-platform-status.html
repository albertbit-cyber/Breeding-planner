Ready for a second laboratory, not yet ready to deploy

The platform now treats each laboratory as its own tenant. A vendor arrives only by invitation, sets its own password, publishes its own tests at its own prices, manages its own staff, and issues documents under its own name. The boundary

Three parties touch laboratory work, and the product rule is that the middle one owns its own section. Nothing crosses between two laboratories, and the administrator sees everything without being able to change it.

Platform Administrator
→ sends the invitation
→ reads everything
→ suspends or reactivates

The only door into the platform, and the only switch that turns a laboratory off.

Tenant Vendor Laboratory
  • Its own tests and prices
  • Its own staff and roles
  • Its own name, address and logo
  • Its own orders, results and certificates

A second laboratory is a second lane. Neither can read the other.

Customer Breeder
→ picks a laboratory
→ sees only its tests
→ is quoted only its prices

The choice comes first. Everything afterwards follows from it.

When a laboratory asks for an order that is not theirs, the answer is “not found”, not “not allowed”. Confirming that a record exists somewhere else is itself a small leak, so the system declines to confirm it.

How a laboratory joins

There is exactly one route in. There is no sign-up page for the Lab Portal and there must never be one — the build fails if a registration route reappears.

  1. The administrator sends an invitation

    Laboratory name and email, plus an optional contact and location that pre-fill the vendor's profile later. A reason is required and goes to the audit log. Nothing is created yet — an unaccepted invitation leaves no half-built laboratory behind.

    POST /api/admin/vendor-labs/invites

  2. The laboratory receives an emailed link

    The link carries a single-use token that expires in fourteen days. Only a hash of it is stored, so the invitation cannot be recovered from the database. Re-inviting the same address withdraws the earlier invitation, so an old link can never quietly still work.

  3. They set their own password

    The acceptance page shows who was invited, to what, and until when — and nothing else. The administrator never sees, sets, or handles the laboratory's credentials.

    GET /api/invites/:token · POST /api/invites/:token/accept

  4. The laboratory comes into existence, all at once

    One transaction creates the organization, the owner's membership, the laboratory record and its own pricing. Because the invitation was delivered to that address and redeemed from it, the email is already verified. If two people click the same link, exactly one succeeds.

  5. They publish what they sell

    The laboratory adds its tests — either written from scratch or copied from the platform's shared library as a starting point — sets its prices, fills in its address and logo, and invites its colleagues. Once it lists itself in the directory, breeders can order.

The administrator's console

Full sight, one switch. This is deliberate: the administrator supports vendors and can shut off a bad actor, but a laboratory's work is its own.

Can
  • List every laboratory with its member, test and order counts
  • Open one and read its members, orders, results, tests and prices
  • Read its invitation history and full audit trail
  • Invite a new laboratory, and withdraw an invitation
  • Suspend a laboratory, and reactivate it
Cannot
  • Change a laboratory's name, address or logo
  • Add, remove or re-role its staff
  • Edit its tests or its prices
  • Touch a result, a certificate or an order's contents
  • Sign in as the laboratory

The restriction is structural rather than a rule someone has to remember: the endpoints an administrator would need in order to do those things do not exist. Suspension is the single write, it always requires a written reason, and it is fully reversible — it stops sign-in and hides the laboratory from breeders without altering any of its data.

Roles inside a laboratory

A person's role in their laboratory is separate from what kind of account they hold on the platform. This is what makes a laboratory with more than one employee possible at all — access follows membership, not the single person the administrator originally invited.

Billing manager currently behaves as a member, because vendor laboratories are not billed. The role exists for the subscription phase and is inert until then.

Removing someone ends their access and nothing else. Their account survives, and so do the results they signed off — a person leaving a laboratory is not a reason to rewrite the record of work they did there.

The breeder's side

Ordering now begins with a choice of laboratory, because there is no longer such a thing as “the catalogue” or “the price list” — only a given laboratory's.

  1. Choose a laboratory

    The directory lists laboratories that are approved, active, and have listed themselves. Each shows its location, how many tests it offers and its typical turnaround.

    GET /api/lab/directory

  2. Pick tests for each animal

    The test list belongs to the chosen laboratory. Suggested tests for possible-het genetics are matched against that laboratory's own offerings.

  3. See a live price

    Quoted from the chosen laboratory's tier pricing. If that laboratory has not finished setting up its prices, the quote fails plainly rather than falling back to somebody else's numbers.

    POST /api/lab/orders/calculate-price

  4. Submit, and print labels

    The order names its laboratory, and is numbered in that laboratory's own sequence. Shipping labels are addressed to that laboratory, using its address — not a fixed one.

  5. Receive results and a certificate

    Issued under the name and logo of the laboratory that actually ran the test. Confirmed results feed back into the animal's recorded genetics.

Changing laboratory mid-order clears the staged animals. Those test selections belong to the previous laboratory's catalogue and would mean nothing in the new one — so the app says so, rather than silently carrying across identifiers that no longer resolve.

The migration

Applying a migration to an empty database only proves the SQL parses. A repeatable harness now builds a throwaway database, applies every earlier migration, seeds the state a real deployment is actually in — one laboratory, the shared catalogue and pricing it sold against, an order placed through it — applies the change on top, and asserts what happened to the data.

Writing it found a defect that would have failed on deploy: the order-line backfill referenced the update target from inside a join condition, which PostgreSQL rejects outright. That is now fixed and verified.

./scripts/verify-lab-tenancy-migration.ps1

What is missing

Four of the five items raised in the first review are closed. What remains is one deployment gate and one deliberate deferral.

The migration has not been rehearsed against live data

Blocks deploy Needs your access

Seeded data only exercises the single-laboratory case. A copy of production exercises real row volume and the branch that deliberately leaves orders unattributed when there is no single laboratory to attribute them to. This is now one command with a runbook that says exactly what to look for — it needs database credentials rather than more engineering.

./scripts/rehearse-migration-on-production-copy.ps1 -SourceUrl "…"

The breeder side is deliberately not organization-scoped yet

Deferred, with cause

Laboratories are tenants; breeder collections still belong to individuals. The reason for holding is specific rather than general: the change touches 125 ownership checks, most of them on the cloud-sync path — which currently carries an unexplained data-loss defect that has already cost one animal record and whose cause is still unknown.

It is also worth being clear that the change delivers nothing visible today. Every breeder already has a personal organization with exactly one member, so organization-scoping is behaviourally identical to the ownership check it replaces. Its value is unlocking breeder teams later. Weighing an invisible change against an active data-loss investigation in the same subsystem, the sequencing is worth keeping.

A way for a laboratory to ask to join

Built

An application form that creates a lead and nothing else — no account, no organization, no token, no access. The response says only that it arrived, so it cannot be used to probe who has applied. Reviewing records a decision and never issues an invitation as a side effect, so access cannot be granted by tidying a queue.

Two things found along the way

Fixed

The migration contained a defect that would have failed on deploy — the order-line backfill referenced the update target from inside a join condition, which PostgreSQL rejects. It was found by writing the verification the migration never had.

And the genetics engine had no tests at all. All three copies of punnett.test.ts contained a single empty export — a stub named as if it were coverage, for the module this entire product computes. It now has twelve, covering inheritance ratios, independent assortment and possible-het uncertainty. All passed first run; the engine was right, nothing was checking.
